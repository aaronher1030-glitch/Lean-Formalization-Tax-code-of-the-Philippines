# Cross-checking the BIR calculator against the model

**What the Bureau of Internal Revenue actually publishes, how it was compared with the formalized schedule, and the four places where they diverge — two arithmetic, two readings of the form.**

Prepared 28 November 2026. Companion to `DEEP_RESEARCH_DISTRIBUTED_TAX_FORMALIZATION.md` and `NIRC_CLUSTER_GRAPH.md`. The measurements are in `data/bir_calculator_check.csv`, regenerated by `python -X utf8 tools/bir_calculator_check.py`; that file is authoritative and the table below is a snapshot of it.

---

## 1. What "the BIR calculator" is

The Bureau publishes exactly one public calculator for computing tax:
**https://www.bir.gov.ph/wtcalculator** — the *Withholding Tax Calculator*, whose engine is `web-services.bir.gov.ph/tax_calculator/wt_calculator.html`, with the arithmetic in `js/wt_calculator.js`.

It is narrower than its reputation. It computes **withholding tax for employees earning purely compensation income**, for one payroll period at a time. It does not compute:

- the annual income tax of a self-employed person, a professional, or a mixed-income earner;
- the 8% optional regime or the ₱3,000,000 VAT threshold (§24(A)(2)(b), §109(CC));
- percentage tax (§116), penalties or interest (§248, §249);
- anything for a corporation.

So the overlap with this repository is **one provision, §24(A)(2)(a)**, reached through a compensation-income bridge: the graduated table applied to taxable compensation for a period. That is enough to test, and it is the only part of the Code a citizen can currently check against government software without filing a return.

**This is itself a finding for the proposal.** The one tax computation the government lets the public run is the one an employer runs on an employee. The 8% election, the self-employed, mixed income, penalties and interest — the parts where a wrong number is hardest for a taxpayer to challenge — have no public calculator at all.

## 2. The method, and where it comes from

Lexifina (`lexifina.com`, `github.com/lexifina/global-law`, CC0) formalized the income tax of 197 jurisdictions in Lean and validated each model in three stages:

1. **official published figures** — tax tables, thresholds, worked examples;
2. **the government's own software** — their calculators and production programs;
3. **independent open-source implementations** — PolicyEngine, GETTSIM, OpenFisca.

Every disagreement is settled by reading the statute, never by adjusting the model until the numbers agree. Their IRS findings were confirmed "by typing households into irs.gov itself". Their conclusion, which this exercise reproduces at one-section scale, is worth quoting because it is the whole thesis:

> Where governments compute by formula, they are right. … Tables generated from a rule do not drift, because when the rule changes the whole table is generated again. The errors sit at the joins.

This document is stage 2 for one jurisdiction and one provision. It is not a replication of their corpus: Lexifina's `global-law` already holds a `philippines/` corpus of dated statute text; what is added here is the comparison against the Bureau's running code, which their public write-ups do not report for the Philippines.

## 3. How the comparison was run

Four numbers per case, and the point is that none of them is a third implementation of the tax law:

| Column | What it is |
|---|---|
| `bir_centavos` | The shipped calculator's answer, **replayed from `wt_calculator.js`** branch by branch, in the same float arithmetic and the same order of operations as the page. |
| `statute_centavos` | The same period table applied to **total** taxable compensation in exact integer centavos, truncating. For the annual table this is `bracketTax` from the Lean model, imported and asserted equal on 11,590 points. |
| `display_net_taxable_centavos` | The **Net Taxable Compensation Income the form itself displays**, replayed from `getTaxableCompensationIncome` and `getNetTaxableCompensationIncome`. Nine live values are pinned in `OBSERVED_DISPLAY` and checked every run, so finding C is measured from the running form rather than read from its source. |
| `OBSERVED` | 30 answers read from the live script in a browser on 28 November 2026 by evaluating the shipped file against a stub DOM. The replay must reproduce all of them or the tool exits non-zero. |

The two observed rows are the anti-drift device: the transcription is checked against the running code every run, so a replay that quietly stops matching the page fails loudly instead of producing a tidy, wrong table.

Period tables are applied to the period's own total taxable compensation. The annual column is a claim about the Code (§24(A)(2)(a)). The daily, weekly, semi-monthly and monthly columns are claims about Annex E of RR 11-2018, the Bureau's own withholding tables, and are now checked against it: the regulation's 20 rows are transcribed in `ANNEX_E` and the tool exits non-zero if `TABLES` departs from them. Two independent reprints of the same table are checked the same way (`REPRINTS`), so the twenty bases do not rest on the annex file either.

## 4. What matches

34 cases, 27 identical. The identical ones are not filler:

- **All five annual brackets and all five bases**: ₱250,000 → 0; ₱400,000 → ₱22,500; ₱800,000 → ₱102,500; ₱2,000,000 → ₱402,500; ₱8,000,000 → ₱2,202,500; and ₱8,000,000.10 → ₱2,202,500.03. Every floor, the first centavo above it, and the top band.
- **The four payroll-period tables, against Annex E.** Each base equals the cumulative tax at its own floor to the centavo — 61.65, 280.85, 1,102.60, 6,034.30 daily, and the same arithmetic monthly, semi-monthly and weekly — so they are consistent tables, not typos. They are also the regulation's tables: all 20 bands match Annex E of RR 11-2018 (the copy the Bureau hosts at `bir-cdn.bir.gov.ph/local/pdf/Annex E RR 11-2018.pdf`, effective 1 January 2023 onwards) on every floor, prescribed base and marginal rate. `ANNEX_E` in `tools/bir_calculator_check.py` holds that transcription, the run exits non-zero if `TABLES` drifts from it, and each base is recomputed from the band below, so a mistyped base fails whether or not the two transcriptions agree.
- **The ₱90,000 ceiling** on 13th month pay and other benefits: at the ceiling, nothing is added; ₱5,000 over it is taxed at 15% (₱750.00).
- **Every floor tested alone**: the bracket below the floor gives 0, the first centavo above it gives the base.

That transcription has further sources, so it does not rest on one reading of one annex. The regulation body (`RR No. 11-2018.pdf`) does four useful things: it sends compensation paid from 1 January 2023 to Annex “E” and 2018–2022 compensation to Annex “D” (§2.79(B)(1)); it states the rule the tuples encode — *“add the tax predetermined in the compensation range … and the rate of tax on the excess of the total compensation over the minimum of the compensation range”* (§2.79(B)(4), Step 4), which also settles that the “over P X” figure is the band's floor rather than a separate threshold; it prints the 2023-onwards annual schedule (§2.79(B)(5)(b)), which is the annual column here exactly; and it names six per-period floors in its worked illustrations — ₱2,192 daily, ₱7,692 weekly, ₱10,417 semi-monthly, ₱20,833, ₱33,333 and ₱66,667 monthly. `RR_TEXT_ANNUAL` and `RR_TEXT_FLOORS` hold those, and `rr_text_mismatches()` checks them every run.

Those same illustrations are what checks the **other tranche**. Annex “D” — compensation paid 1 January 2018 to 31 December 2022, the Bureau's `bir-cdn.bir.gov.ph/local/pdf/Annex D RR 11-2018.pdf` — is transcribed separately in `ANNEX_D` and kept out of `TABLES`, because the calculator implements only the 2023 table; nothing here runs a case through 2018 law. It is checked against the regulation body rather than against another copy of itself, and all six 2018 bases the body prints match: ₱356.16 on the daily ₱2,192 band (30%), ₱576.92 weekly on ₱7,692 (25%), ₱0.00 semi-monthly on ₱10,417 (20%), ₱0.00 monthly on ₱20,833 (20%), ₱2,500.00 on ₱33,333 (25%) and ₱10,833.33 on ₱66,667 (30%). `RR_ILLUSTRATIONS` holds them, `annex_d_mismatches()` checks base, rate and floor every run with its own failure path, and it also requires Annex D's floors to be Annex E's — they are identical, because the ₱250,000 exemption did not move between the tranches, so only the bases and the rates changed.

Annex D gets no recomputation check, and that is a measurement rather than an omission: its bases are **not** the tax accumulated at their own floor. Applying the arithmetic that holds to the centavo on Annex E, ten of Annex D's sixteen computed bases are off — by one centavo at the daily ₱82.19, three at ₱356.16, 21 at ₱1,342.47 and ₱6,602.74, and between two and 19 centavos on each weekly band — which is what annualising and rounding produces, where the 2023 table was generated from the annual schedule in one step. `annex_d_drift()` reports the drift on every run and asserts nothing about it. Two caveats on the source: the annex file's text layer mangles the monthly row as “0.00 0.0”, and Illustration 9's prose adds to ₱43,659.89 where its own schedule adds to ₱41,833.23 (with a ₱103,833 excess where its figures give ₱103,333). Neither touches a prescribed base, but they are why every figure above is read from the schedule line, not the narrative.

What the body does not do is reprint the 2023 per-period **bases**. Its illustrations compute 2018 tax, so they carry Annex “D” bases and can confirm floors only. Those twenty bases are therefore checked against two publications that are not the annex file:

- **The Bureau's own withholding-tax page**, `bir.gov.ph/index.php/tax-information/withholding-tax.html`, as print-captured into Annex A of **OCA Circular No. 35-2024** (Office of the Court Administrator, 5 February 2024). The circular adopts “the Revised Withholding Tax Table (Annex A) as required by the Bureau” for the monthly salaries of court employees, and its Annex A is a capture of the Bureau's page — the page's URL in the footer, its “EFFECTIVE DATE JANUARY 1, 2023” annual table, and all. The page itself returned 404 on 28 November 2026, so the circular is the surviving copy checked here.
- **Vialto Partners' 2024 year-end employment-tax alert** (4 December 2024), which reprints the same table as “effective January 1, 2023 onwards”.

Both print all twenty bases, and both match `TABLES` band for band: 61.65, 280.85, 1,102.60, 6,034.30 daily; 432.60, 1,971.20, 7,740.45, 42,355.65 weekly; 937.50, 4,270.70, 16,770.70, 91,770.70 semi-monthly; 1,875.00, 8,541.80, 33,541.80, 183,541.80 monthly. `REPRINTS` in `tools/bir_calculator_check.py` holds both and `reprint_mismatches()` checks every band on every run, with its own failure path.

A caveat about copies: not every third-party reprint is usable. A payroll vendor's help-desk article headed “Effective January 1, 2023, and onwards” prints the 2023 ranges with the 2018 Annex “D” bases (₱82.19, ₱356.16, ₱1,342.47, ₱6,602.74) and a 32% band, and links to a “revised” copy that does the same. That is why the corroboration here is per band from sources that print the schedule consistently, not a citation count.

What the two reprints settle and what they do not: a transcription error in the annex file — the kind its mangled daily top base, “P 6,034.00.30”, invites — is now ruled out. A drafting error in the regulation is not, because every one of these sources reprints the same table rather than deriving it from §24(A)(2)(a); that derivation remains the practitioner question in §7.

So the formula is right. The schedule in this repository and the schedule in the Bureau's calculator are the same function everywhere tested — which is exactly the result that makes the seven divergences meaningful rather than noise.

The four added cases are the two SSS pairs. Each agrees with the statute on the figures actually entered, so they add no divergence; what they add is the measurement for finding C — the same salary entered with and without the SSS box, and entered already net of the contribution.

## 5. What does not match

Reproduced from `data/bir_calculator_check.csv`, 28 November 2026.

| Case | Period | BIR | Model | Off by |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `daily_zero_600` | daily | ₱600.00 | ₱0.00 | +₱600.00 |
| `daily_zero_684_99` | daily | ₱684.99 | ₱0.00 | +₱684.99 |
| `daily_zero_with_supp` | daily | ₱1,600.00 | ₱162.45 | +₱1,437.55 |
| `monthly_below_floor_with_supp` | monthly | ₱0.00 | ₱1,375.05 | −₱1,375.05 |
| `annual_250000_10` | annual | ₱0.02 | ₱0.01 | +₱0.01 |
| `annual_cross_399999_supp100k` | annual | ₱37,499.85 | ₱42,499.80 | −₱4,999.95 |
| `annual_below_floor_with_supp` | annual | ₱0.00 | ₱6,000.00 | −₱6,000.00 |

Four divergences, in descending order of what they cost a person.

### A. The daily table returns income as tax

`getDailyZero` ends with

```js
else if (wtTaxableCompensationIncome >= 0)
{
    wtTotalTaxWithheld=((wtTaxableCompensationIncome - 0) + wtSupplementary);
    return wtTotalTaxWithheld;
}
```

No rate, no base. For taxable regular pay below ₱685 a day the function returns **the pay itself**, not zero. The other four period functions return `0` in the same branch, which is what makes this a typo rather than a rule.

A daily-paid worker at ₱600/day is shown ₱600.00 of withholding tax per day — all of it. With ₱1,000 of supplementary pay the same worker is shown ₱1,600.00 against a statutory ₱162.45.

### B. Supplementary pay is taxed at regular pay's rate

The bracket is chosen from `getTotalRegular()` alone, and supplementary is then added *inside* that bracket's excess:

```js
wtTotalTaxWithheld = (((wtTaxableCompensationIncome - floor) + wtSupplementary) * rate) + base;
```

Two consequences, both arithmetic:

- **When the total crosses a bracket, the crossing is ignored.** ₱399,999 regular plus ₱100,000 supplementary is ₱499,999 of taxable compensation, which is in the 20% band: ₱42,499.80. The calculator picks the 15% band from ₱399,999 and returns ₱37,499.85 — **₱4,999.95 short**.
- **When regular pay is below the first floor, the supplementary disappears entirely.** ₱20,000 a month plus ₱10,000 commission: the branch is `>= 0`, which returns 0, so the ₱10,000 is never taxed — **₱1,375.05 short**. Annually, ₱240,000 regular plus ₱50,000 supplementary produces ₱0.00 instead of ₱6,000.00.

The calculator's own form contradicts itself here, and now measurably: ₱20,000 regular plus ₱10,000 commission displays **₱30,000.00** as Net Taxable Compensation Income and taxes **₱0.00**, while ₱399,999 plus ₱100,000 displays **₱499,999.00** and taxes it as if the total were ₱399,999.

### C. The SSS box reaches neither the tax nor the display

The fieldset is headed *Non-Taxable/Exempt Compensation Income* and contains *"SSS, GSIS, PAG-IBIG Contributions and Union Dues (Employee's Share Only)"*. The tool now records the form's own displayed **Net Taxable Compensation Income**, replayed from `getTaxableCompensationIncome` and `getNetTaxableCompensationIncome` and pinned to live values, so this finding is measured:

| Salary entered | SSS box | Displayed net taxable | Tax |
|---|---|---|---|
| ₱30,000 monthly | — | ₱30,000.00 | ₱1,375.05 |
| ₱30,000 monthly | ₱1,350 | ₱30,000.00 | ₱1,375.05 |
| ₱28,650 monthly | — | ₱28,650.00 | ₱1,172.55 |
| ₱400,000 annual | — | ₱400,000.00 | ₱22,500.00 |
| ₱400,000 annual | ₱10,000 | ₱400,000.00 | ₱22,500.00 |
| ₱390,000 annual | — | ₱390,000.00 | ₱21,000.00 |

Entering the contribution changes nothing, on screen or in the answer. `getPayrollPeriod()` returns 1 for annual and 12, 24, 66 or 264 for the payroll periods, so its `< 4` test is true only for annual: the payroll branches subtract SSS and the display branch adds it straight back, while the annual branch never subtracts it. Both paths land on total compensation, whatever the box says.

What remains a reading is what the form *asks for*. The salary box is not labelled "net of mandatory deductions", so the same ₱30,000 salary has two defensible entries:

- ₱30,000 as basic salary with ₱1,350 in the SSS box → **₱1,375.05**;
- the salary already net of the contribution, ₱28,650 → **₱1,172.55**.

**₱202.50 a month at ₱30,000, ₱1,500.00 a year at ₱400,000**, with the form displaying a different base in the two fillings and no indication of which one it taxed. Whether the box is meant to be a deduction is for a practitioner and RR 11-2018; the measurement is that the box is inert.

### D. The answer depends on binary floating point

The page computes in JavaScript numbers and rounds once at the end: `Math.round(x * 100) / 100`. The model computes in exact centavos.

- 15% of 10 centavos is exactly 1.5. The page returns **₱0.02**; the statute truncating gives **₱0.01**.
- 35% of 10 centavos is exactly 3.5. The page returns **3**, not 4 — because `10 * 0.35` is `3.4999999999999996` in binary. It lands on the right centavo by luck.

One centavo is nothing. It is also the same class of fault Lexifina found in GOV.UK's calculator, where floating point turned 29% into 28.999…% and produced a £630 figure where the statute gives £652. And it is the reason this repository's checkers take integer centavos and truncate: an assessment whose last centavo depends on the last bit is not reproducible, and an irreproducible assessment is not contestable.

## 6. What this is worth to the proposal

Three things, none of which is "BIR is wrong".

1. **The method works at one-section scale.** A model of one provision, plus the Bureau's own published code, produced a specific, reproducible, peso-denominated finding in an afternoon. That is the pilot: not a national audit, a demonstration that the loop closes.
2. **The finding type is the pitch.** Not "your software is broken" but *"this branch returns income as tax, here is the case, here is the statute, here is the fix, and here is a test that stays green"*. It is a bug report a maintainer can act on — which is what Lexifina filed against the IRS, GOV.UK and HMRC, with reproduction steps.
3. **The gap is the ask.** The public calculator covers salaried employees. The 8% election, mixed income, and the penalty and interest provisions are the ones a taxpayer most needs to check and cannot. That is the concrete thing BetterGov could open up.

It is also the cheapest possible demonstration of the two-key rule in `DEEP_RESEARCH_DISTRIBUTED_TAX_FORMALIZATION.md` §4.1: everything above is a *kernel-and-arithmetic* result, and none of it is a legal conclusion until a practitioner signs the reading — including the reading that supplementary compensation belongs in the base.

## 7. Limits

- **It is one provision, one page, one date.** `wt_calculator.js` as served on 28 November 2026. If the Bureau changes the page the replay drifts, and the tool will say so rather than pass.
- **The annual column is the Code; the other four are the regulation.** The four payroll-period tables were checked against Annex E of RR 11-2018 line for line and match (§4), so agreement with the regulation is no longer an open question here. Two things sit outside that check. First, whether Annex E's per-period floors are the correct derivation from §24(A)(2)(a) rather than a whole-peso rounding of it is a regulatory question for a practitioner — the daily floor of ₱685 against the annual ₱250,000 divided by 365 is the visible join. Second, the 2018–2022 tranche: it is now transcribed as Annex “D” and checked against the six 2018 bases RR 11-2018 prints in its illustrations, all of which match (§4), but **fourteen of its twenty bases still rest on the annex file alone**, and the table has no arithmetic check to fall back on because its own bases are rounded rather than cumulative. A second source for those fourteen, and a reprint of Annex D to compare against, are the obvious next contributions. No source supplies an independent *derivation* of any of these tables: every copy reprints the same schedule rather than computing it from §24(A)(2)(a), so a drafting error in Annex E or Annex D itself would look identical in all of them.
- **Two findings turn on a reading.** C now measures what the form does — the SSS box changes neither the tax nor the displayed base — but whether the box is *meant* to be a deduction, and B's premise that supplementary compensation belongs in the taxable base, are readings of the form and of RR 11-2018 that a practitioner should confirm before either is filed.
- **The minimum wage earner text was not tested.** The page states that if an MWE receives any taxable additional compensation, "the withholding tax due will be automatically computed by this calculator based on the entire income earnings (both non-taxable and taxable compensation income)". Read literally that taxes the exempt minimum wage. This is flagged, not claimed — it needs the RR text and a practitioner.
- **No real taxpayer data.** Every case is a constructed boundary case. Nothing here is an assessment, and none of it is legal advice.
